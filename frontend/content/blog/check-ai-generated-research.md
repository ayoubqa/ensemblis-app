A market analysis that would once have taken a week now takes a few minutes to generate. It arrives well written, confidently argued and full of citations. That is exactly the problem: the qualities that make AI-generated research persuasive are not the qualities that make it true.

This article gives you five checks to run, in order, before you act on research an AI tool produced — whether you generated it yourself, a colleague did, or a vendor delivered it. None of them requires technical knowledge. Most take minutes.

> [!KEY]
> Check in proportion to the decision. A reversible, low-cost choice can rest on a quick read. A decision that is expensive to undo deserves all five checks — and the third one most of all.

## Why AI research needs checking at all

Language models generate text that is *plausible*; whether it is *accurate* is a separate question. The best-known symptom is invented sources. In a 2023 study, two researchers asked ChatGPT to write short literature reviews on 42 topics and then checked all 636 citations it produced. With GPT-3.5, 55% of the citations were fabricated — they did not exist. With GPT-4, 18% were. And among the citations that did exist, 43% (GPT-3.5) and 24% (GPT-4) contained substantive errors.[^walters]

Those were 2023 models answering from memory. Newer tools that search the web as they write can link to real pages — but a real page can still be misquoted, out of date or beside the point, which is why the third check below exists. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology lists *confabulation* — confidently presenting false or erroneous content — among the risks that generative AI creates or makes worse, in its guidance for organisations.[^nist] Treat it as a property of the technology, not a bug that was fixed last year.

## The five checks

![Five checks before acting on AI-generated research: fit, trace, source, gaps and coherence. ### 1. Fit: does it answer the question you asked?

Write your question down in one sentence before you read the answer. Then compare. AI tools are good at answering a *nearby, easier* question: asked which of three markets to enter first, a tool may describe all three markets well and never actually rank them. Asked about your customers, it may describe customers in your industry in general.

If the conclusion doesn't answer your question directly, nothing else in the document matters yet.

### 2. Trace: can each important claim be traced to a source?

Go through the conclusion and the figures it rests on. Every number, company name, date and quotation that the recommendation depends on should point to a specific source. "Industry reports suggest…" is not a source. Neither is a list of references at the end that isn't tied to individual claims.

Claims that can't be traced aren't necessarily wrong — but they are untested, and you should mark them that way in your own head before you repeat them in a meeting.

### 3. Source: does the source exist, and does it say that?

This is the check that catches fabricated citations, and the one most people skip. For the claims your decision rests on, open the source and find the sentence or the table that supports the claim. You are looking for four failure modes:

- **The source doesn't exist** — the link is dead or leads somewhere unrelated, or the report can't be found under that title.
- **The source exists but says something else** — a different figure, a different year, a different region.
- **The source is real but weak** — a vendor's marketing page cited as if it were independent research, or a forecast cited as if it were a measurement.
- **The source is out of date** — accurate when published, superseded since.

You don't need to check every citation. Check the ones the conclusion depends on.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A hypothetical, for illustration. An analysis says: "The European market for commercial heat pumps grew 23% last year [3]." Source [3] turns out to be a manufacturers' association press release about *residential* sales in *three* countries, from *two* years ago. Each part of the claim is close to something real; together, the claim is unsupported. This is what a fabricated or garbled citation usually looks like — not obviously absurd, just slightly wrong in several directions at once.

### 4. Gaps: what is missing that could change the conclusion?

Ask what a sceptical expert would ask. Which competitors, regions or customer types weren't considered? How old is the newest data? What would have to be true for the opposite recommendation to be right — and did the research look for it?

A useful habit is to ask the tool itself, in a fresh session, to argue against the conclusion. You are not asking it to be right; you are asking it to show you where to look.

### 5. Coherence: do the parts agree?

Read the figures, the findings and the recommendation side by side. Do the numbers add up? Does the recommendation follow from the findings, or did the summary become more confident than the evidence? Are the caveats in the body contradicted by the executive summary? Inconsistencies are cheap to spot and often reveal where a model filled a gap with something plausible.

## How much checking does a decision deserve?

| The decision | Examples | Checks |
|---|---|---|
| Cheap and reversible | Choosing which topics to explore further; a first draft for internal discussion | 1 and 2 |
| Costly or public | A pricing change; a claim in a sales deck or on your website | 1–3 for every claim you'll repeat |
| Expensive to undo | Entering a market; hiring for a new function; a supplier you'll depend on | All five, with sources opened by a person |

The cost of checking is minutes. The cost of a confident mistake in the third row is months.

## What to ask of a tool or a vendor

If you regularly receive AI-made research, make these checks easier by asking for research that is built to be checked:

- **Claims tied to sources**, numbered in the text — not a reference list at the end.
- **A statement of what wasn't established**: open questions, weak evidence, data that couldn't be found.
- **The question restated at the top**, with the criteria the answer should meet, so you can run check 1 in seconds.
- **Dates on everything** — of the sources and of the research itself.
